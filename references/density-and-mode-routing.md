# Density and Mode Routing

Use this reference to choose how visually sparse or rich a design should be.

The governing rule is:

> Concept decides density.

Do not make every poster minimal.
Do not make every poster dense.

## Step 1 — Identify the job

Ask what the poster must do:
- stop scrolling
- explain
- announce
- persuade
- create curiosity
- strengthen brand perception
- tell a story
- drive action

## Step 2 — Choose the primary mode

### Conceptual Key Visual
Choose when one image can communicate the message.

Typical density: low to medium.

### Typographic Concept
Choose when a title or keyword has strong visual potential.

Typical density: low.

### Editorial Minimal
Choose for premium, cultural, thoughtful, research, academic, or reflective content.

Typical density: low.

### Prop / Environmental Story
Choose when the story is revealed through objects and environment.

Typical density: medium to high.

### Brand-led Promotional
Choose when action, brand presence, or one/two supporting facts are important.

Typical density: medium.

### Informational Poster
Choose only when multiple facts must be visible in-image.

Typical density: medium to high, but highly structured.

## Step 3 — Density budget

### Low density
Use roughly:
- 1 hero
- 0–1 support object
- 1 headline
- 0–1 support line

### Medium density
Use roughly:
- 1 hero
- 2–4 subordinate context elements
- headline
- 1–2 small information zones

### High density
Allowed when the narrative or information requires it.

Requirements:
- one dominant focal area
- strong grouping
- consistent visual language
- clear scan path
- no equal-weight clutter

## Step 4 — Choose the correct amount of whitespace

Whitespace should match the intended tone.

More whitespace often suits:
- premium
- luxury
- academic
- conceptual
- cultural
- reflective

Less whitespace may suit:
- energetic campaigns
- narrative scenes
- entertainment
- expressive editorial work
- prop stories

But even dense designs need internal breathing room.

## Step 5 — Information compression

For each fact in the source brief, classify it as:

### Must be in image
Needed for immediate understanding or action.

### Can be implied visually
Better communicated through the concept.

### Caption-only
Useful detail, but not necessary for the visual.

Do not let source-text length dictate visual density.

## Step 6 — Stop conditions

Stop adding when:
- the hierarchy is already clear
- the story is understandable
- an additional object would duplicate meaning
- a box exists only because there is space
- a decorative element competes with the hero

## Example: hackathon

A source brief may include:
- date
- team size
- tracks
- submission platform
- prize
- certificate
- URL

Conceptual mode might keep only:
- event name
- visual metaphor
- date

Informational mode might keep:
- title
- hero
- date
- 2–4 grouped information modules
- CTA

The correct choice depends on the user's visual objective, not the amount of text supplied.
